On February 12, 2021, Shopify was trading at ~$1,500/share, an all-time high for the
stock. From February 12, 2016 to the day it reached its record high, Shopify generated
7,429.7% for its shareholders (source: KoyFin). I thought to myself, wait, is that ~75x in
5 years? I was so incredulous that I had to take my calculator out and manually type it
to confirm it is indeed ~75x in 5 years.
A publicly listed company based out of Ottawa (from where I am writing this piece
myself) probably beat the supermajority of successful venture capital investments
during the same time. Ironically, BVP in their original Shopify investment memo
mentioned “very few VCs believed that software businesses targeting SMBs were good
investments”
.
I have read probably ~50 articles/substacks/blogs/podcasts/sell-side research on
Shopify in the last one month, but perhaps nobody did as compelling and complete a
job of covering Shopify as “The 10th man”
. I read his ~11k-worded piece not once, but
twice. I then had a two-hour long call with him to go through his model and his current
thoughts on Shopify. As a testament to my name “MBI”
, I have quite liberally borrowed
ideas from him and many others, and then made an earnest attempt to see the long-
term future of Shopify.

Section 1: Shopify Business Model

Before Shopify was founded in 2006, Shopify Co-founder and current CEO Tobi Lutke
built an online store to sell snowboards in 2004 and he quickly realized how
challenging it was to build an e-commerce store to sell things online. The company
then pivoted from an online store to an e-commerce platform that provides a SaaS
solution enabling anyone with a credit card to start selling online. Basically, anyone
with a sense of entrepreneurial firepower can start selling things online in a matter of
hours and in Shopify’s jargon, it wants to “arm these rebels”
. The only thing these
“rebels” need to do is master the core craft, create demand for the products they are
selling, and utilize Shopify’s tools to build a direct relationship with their customers
online.
Shopify had enjoyed three distinct tailwinds in the last decade and half: the secular
growth of e-commerce, the consumerization of enterprise software, and a golden
decade for Direct-to-Customer (DTC) channel focused brands/companies. In many
ways, Shopify not only enjoyed these tailwinds, but it also turbocharged the wind even
faster with superior product and topnotch execution year after year. It is no wonder
Shopify was able to grow its topline at 70.2% CAGR or ~14x (I kid you not) in the last
five years.
Before doing this deep dive, I always sort of wondered how Shopify could possibly
grow at such a rate for so long and why there is no compelling neck-on-neck
alternative when the product itself seems quite plain vanilla at first glance.
Benedict Evans in a recent post on Shopify alluded to this sense of bewilderment when
people think about Shopify and feel bemused how Shopify could build a ~$140 Bn
market cap company solving such basic e-commerce problems. Evans reminisced an
old story related to Dropbox to make a point for Shopify:
“There’s a famous story that when Drew Houston was working on Dropbox, everyone
he showed it to said ‘There are hundreds of these!’ and he always said ‘yes, but which
one do you use?”
So yes, merchants more often than not choose Shopify over other competitors. While
the core product is perhaps somewhat commoditized by now, Shopify has beaten
every other competitor in terms of velocity of the product development and kept
adding new functionalities to stay a step ahead of others. Although subscription

=== PAGE 4 ===
solution was the source for the majority of revenues even five years back, today
Shopify generates most of its revenue from Shopify Payments, its native payment
integration that Shopify merchants can use, and as you will see by the end of this deep
dive, Shopify may possibly generate majority of revenue from its recently launched
fulfilment network in 2030. This velocity of the evolution of the product and the
mindshare Shopify enjoys among SMBs have made it the market leader in today’s e-
commerce platform business.
Before we go into more detail about Shopify, let me take a step back and first discuss
what a platform really is. While many tend to label most internet companies as some
sort of “platform”
, Bill Gates had a very short, crisp way to describe how to identify
whether a company is platform or not: “A platform is when the economic value of
everybody that uses it, exceeds the value of the company that creates it. Then it’s a
platform.
”
Ben Thompson explained how Shopify passes the platform test:
“
…this is what Shopify has already accomplished when it comes to referral partners
(who drive new merchants onto the platform), developers (who build apps for
managing Shopify stores) and theme designers (who sell themes to customize the
look-and-feel of stores).
In other words, Shopify clears the Bill Gates Line — it captures a minority of the value
in the ecosystem it has created — and the Shopify Fulfillment Network should fit right
in:
What is powerful about this model is that it leverages the best parts of modularity —
diversity and competition at different parts of the value chain — and aligns the
incentives of all of them. Every referral partner, developer, theme designer, and now
3PL provider is simultaneously incentivized to compete with each other narrowly and
ensure that Shopify succeeds broadly, because that means the pie is bigger for
everyone.
”
Let me briefly explain how Shopify makes money. Shopify reports its revenue in two
segments: Subscription Solutions and Merchant Solutions.

Subscription Solutions: As you can see below, Shopify has three subscription plans for
SMBs to build an online store: Basic Shopify, Shopify, and Advanced Shopify. To
explore more differences among these plans, go here.
￼
Apart from these three plans, Shopify also has a plan called “Shopify Lite” ($9/month)
which is basically a “buy button” to your existing store, but you cannot build an online
store with this plan.
While Shopify initially targeted SMB merchants in e-commerce, today it has a number
of enterprise clients from the likes of successful DTC brands such as Allbirds to legacy
brick and mortar retailers who want to have e-commerce operations such as Staples.
For these enterprise clients, Shopify Plus was launched which costs $2,000/month or
0.25% of GMV whichever is higher with the maximum price being $40,000/month.
A common concern among bears is the level of churn Shopify experiences in its
subscription solutions which generated 31% of Shopify’s revenue in 2020. This concern
is largely misplaced. Shopify does not disclose its churn related data, but it is quite
conceivable that the basic tier ($29/month) experiences significant churn. BVP in its
initial memo on Shopify indicated the level of churn Shopify typically experiences: “By
month 3, Shopify retains roughly 75% of its customers. By month 12, 50–60% of
customers remain. Once stabilized, monthly churn ranges from 3–5%.” As you can
understand, it’s not the stickiest SaaS solution out there.
The reason this concern is largely misplaced is adequately explained by Ben
Thompson: “I would argue that for Shopify a high churn rate is just as much a positive
signal as it is a negative one: the easier it is to start an e-commerce business on the

=== PAGE 6 ===
platform, the more failures there will be. And, at the same time, the greater likelihood
there will be of capturing and supporting successes.
”
Focusing too much on the churn also doesn’t make quite the mathematical sense. “The
10th man” made a very basic observation to dispel most concerns related to churn:
“price of a Shopify plan is 2.7x the price of the Basic, while the starting price for
Shopify Plus is 67x the price of Basic. If a single Basic customer grew into a Shopify
Plus plan, it would completely offset the lost revenue from 66 small merchants going
out of business.
”
Merchant Solutions: Shopify generates ~70% of its revenue from merchant solutions in
2020, super-majority of which is from Shopify Payments. Launched in 2013 and
currently available in 17 countries, Shopify Payments is a fully integrated payment
processing service that allows merchants to accept and process payment cards online
and offline. Shopify has payment service provider agreements with Stripe
(automatically renews every 12 months unless terminated with 6 months’ notice).
With Shopify Payments, subscribers of Basic, Shopify (standard/middle tier), and
Advanced plans get charged 2.9%+30¢, 2.7%+30¢, 2.4%+30¢ respectively. In 2020,
Shopify Payments penetration was 45.1% of total GMV. In terms of adoption of
merchants, this is how the adoption looked like by country in 2020:

Apart from Shopify Payments, Merchant Solutions also consist of referral fees from
partners, advertising revenue on the Shopify App Store, Shopify Capital, Shop Pay
Installments, Shopify Shipping, Shopify Fulfillment Network, the sale of POS hardware
and collaborative warehouse fulfillment solutions, and Shopify Email.
Almost 90% of Shopify’s merchants use the Shopify AppStore and on average, they
have installed 6 apps (2018 September data). While there are 5,876 apps on Shopify’s
Appstore, only 29 of them are “Made by Shopify”
. Shopify leveraged the third-party
developer ecosystem and let them develop tools for its vibrant merchant ecosystem.
With Shopify Capital, it is providing loans to the merchants to manage their working
capital expenses. Incidentally, since I use Stripe to receive payments for “MBI Deep
Dives”
, I received a loan offer from Stripe Capital a couple of weeks ago and I was
wondering whether Stripe Capital could provide the same offer to Shopify’s merchants.
It turns out due to their agreement, Stripe cannot pursue Shopify’s merchants directly
which effectively leaves Shopify as possibly one of the very few companies who would
have access to lots of relevant data points to take underwriting decisions and offer
loans to their merchants.

=== PAGE 8 ===
In 2019, Shopify acquired 6 River System (6RS) that provides collaborative warehouse
fulfillment solutions. Shopify has partnered with Third-Party Logistics (3PL) companies,
many of whom they might lease these robots to efficiently manage fulfilment. At the
same time, Shopify also recently launched Shopify Fulfilment Network (SFN) in which
they control end-to-end fulfilment but still let merchants control the brand and
customer relationship. While Shopify is still taking somewhat asset-light approach in
building SFN, I think it is quite likely that they will eventually try to build an Amazon-like
end-to-end solution in which they control every step of the process.
Alex Danco in a recent podcast appearance to discuss Shopify explained the essence
of merchant solutions as first and second derivative of its core subscription business:
“So merchants is similarly like, the more capital that you take from us and pay us back,
the more money we make, and then that grows along with the size of your business.
The more products that you buy on Handshake in our wholesale marketplace, the more
money that we take as a cut of that. And so in contrast to Core, which is monetizing
directly, we can say that Merchant Services monetizes the first derivative of customer
success. As our customers become successful, we succeed with them. Now, if you go
to Ecosystem, so if Core monetizes directly and Merchant Services monetizes the first
derivative, Ecosystem monetizes the second derivative of customer success. So as our
customers become successful, the Ecosystem around them grows because there's
GMV and there's work to be done and there's this interesting ecosystem to contribute
to And so we monetize directly to that, right? So it grows as a second function of
merchant growth. So here, again, we have an app store and the app store takes a cut
of what you make on it.
This is a kind of compounding that is very powerful because it is the success of Core
leads to Merchant Services being a good product, which leads to more GMV
happening, which leads to more Ecosystem happening. It is a very, very powerful type
of multi-player compounding. And our job is basically to just take a small but
meaningful cut of that that allows us to keep reinvesting into making it better.
”
Section 2: Exploring the future of retail
The size of the US retail market (excluding food services & drinking places) was $5.6
Trillion in 2020. Given the size of the market, I would like to define the scope of our

=== PAGE 9 ===
discussion first. I will primarily focus on three big questions: a) e-commerce
penetration rate in the long-term, b) the dynamics between DTC and marketplaces,
and c) whether Shopify should build a marketplace.
E-commerce penetration in the long-term
While e-commerce is one of the largest beneficiaries of the pandemic, it has been one
of the most potent secular themes for the last two decades. Covid-19 has certainly
accelerated what has already been going on.
Before talking about the possible long-term penetration, let me set the context.
Although I just mentioned US retail market (excluding food services & drinking places)
size is $5.6 Trillion, we need to adjust a little more to identify the Total Addressable
Market (TAM). Gasoline sales, which is not addressable by e-commerce, is ~$500 Bn. If
we also exclude Automobile & other moto vehicle/parts dealers which is ~$1.5 Trillion,
the adjusted retail sales was $4 Trillion in 2020. Since US e-commerce sales was $792
Bn in 2020, it implies ~20% penetration. As you can see below, while penetration was
steadily increasing 80-150 bps each year before the pandemic, 2020 created a step
function change in e-commerce sector. Of course, part of the ~400 bps increased
penetration is just a factor of slow growth of the denominator since physical stores
were closed or in operation with limited capacity. Although e-commerce was just ~16%
of overall retail sales in 2019, it drove ~77% of the growth in overall retail sales in the
US in 2020.

=== PAGE 10 ===
￼
Since the pandemic effect is expected to subside this year and physical stores will
come back in full capacity, we can perhaps understand why e-commerce penetration’s
increase in 2021 and 2022 will be somewhat muted compared to the last few years.
But JPM clearly expects things to pick up in 2023 and beyond.
I don’t have high confidence on JPM’s (or anyone, including me) ability to forecast
these numbers. I do, however, wonder how much the current pandemic may lead us to
estimate the long-term e-commerce penetration biased upward. I don’t know whether
it’s just me, but part of me sometimes wonder whether we should be a bit
underwhelmed with just ~20% e-commerce penetration in 2020. We were all literally
forced to buy things online by mother nature, and yet 80% of the sales did not happen
online.

Shopify, of course, is a global company. While international (outside Canada, and US)

penetration itself is just ~50% in the developing world.
International market is likely to prove to be of paramount importance for Shopify for
them to deliver what is implied in the current stock price which will be discussed later.

The “Battle Royale” between DTC vs Marketplaces
Broadly speaking, e-commerce can be segmented into four categories: pureplay
marketplaces (Amazon, eBay, Etsy etc.), e-commerce segment of scaled brick & mortar
players (Walmart, Target, Costco, Home Depot etc.), e-commerce segment of subscale
brick & mortar players (Staples, local superstores), Direct-to-Consumer or DTC
segment (traditional CPG brands such as Heinz, General Mills as well as DTC startups
such as Allbirds). For the purpose of this discussion, I will just bucket the first two as
“marketplaces” and the latter two as “DTC” even though there are important nuances
within these buckets. Shopify’s merchants or target market primarily consist of the DTC
segment, but their merchants can also use marketplaces as one of their channels to
sell online, but that’s typically not why you would want to be a Shopify merchant. You
want the customers to buy directly from your website.
Shopify’s success hinges upon its merchants’ success. Even though Shopify is just a
platform selling the tools to its merchants to sell online, Shopify lives (and dies) on how
well its merchants do in convincing customers to buy things directly from them instead

=== PAGE 15 ===
of going to marketplaces. Therefore, even though Shopify does not directly compete
against marketplaces, their merchants do, and their merchants’ fight is essentially their
fight.
In many cases in today’s technology space, competition may seem largely absent or
weak horizontally in a particular market, but in a long enough time frame, many new
competitions may emerge vertically or at least encroach into the juicy economics that
an incumbent enjoys in a particular market.
Benedict Evans put this perfectly,
“This is very often what competitive threats look like
in technology. In markets with strong network effects or winner-takes-most effects, it’s
very hard to displace a new incumbent directly, but pretty common to address an
underlying customer need in another way. So, Google doesn’t think about Bing nearly
as much as it thinks about Amazon and Facebook, and Amazon thinks about Shopify,
because they change what the businesses might be, and offer your customers a
different way to solve their problem.
”
So, let’s talk about marketplace (e.g. Amazon) vs platform enabling army of merchants
(e.g. Shopify).
In 2020, top 100 global online marketplaces sold $2.7 trillion of products. Global e-
commerce retail sales were $4.3 trillion, implying ~60% of e-commerce sales
happening in marketplaces. As you can see below, just the three marketplaces in China
alone generated ~$1.6 trillion of GMV, implying ~90% of Chinese e-commerce taking
place in marketplaces. In the US, it is almost all about Amazon. 1P+3P generated ~38%
of total US e-commerce GMV of ~$792 Bn in 2020. While not as high as 90%, market
share of marketplaces in the US e-commerce perhaps also hovers around ~70-80%.
While there may be many different reasons why marketplace businesses are so
incredibly successful, I want to focus on things in the context of platforms such as
Shopify.

=== PAGE 16 ===
￼
The most successful and powerful marketplace businesses have almost always one
thing in common: it unlocks a new vector of supply. Can we imagine how inferior the
Uber business model would be if it could only allow taxi drivers (the supply in the then
status quo market) to drive for them? Many people who may not want to rent a place to
sell their things in the physical real world are more than willing to take a stab at it
online. The cost of failure is just really, really low (especially compared to 19th and 20th
century world) in the information age. Very few businesses capitalized on this simple
idea better than marketplace businesses.
Marketplaces and platforms unlock different vectors of supply. All the side hustlers or
anyone who thinks they might be good at making something can sign up to sell things
online. If their products don’t sell, they can just continue to do their primary job/career.
Shopify, on the other hand, unlocks mostly new entrepreneurs who may be hindered in
the past because of the difficulty of starting a business from scratch but can start a
new endeavor just in hours now. Marketplace businesses can unlock both these supply,
but Shopify by and large just unlocks the latter. Moreover, while Shopify’s merchants
are all trying to acquire sometime similar customers independently, there is hardly any
network effects among them. One merchant’s success does not contribute to any other

=== PAGE 17 ===
merchant’s success. The dynamic is quite different in a marketplace. Since marketplace
is essentially a network of millions of buyers and sellers in the same place, we know
from Metcalfe's law that value of a network is proportional to the square of the number
of connected users of the system (n^2).
When we look at numbers, it does exhibit these dynamics. There are 9.8 million sellers
in Amazon marketplace. Even Etsy, which generated only $10 Bn GMV in 2020, had 4.4
million sellers in its marketplace. Despite all its successes, Shopify currently has ~1.8
mn merchants on its platform.
Of course, the merchants who are successful on Shopify’s platform are able to build
much more resilient business model than sellers in Amazon marketplace. But it almost
feels like a “rich man’s problem” to me. If you are primarily building DTC brand
leveraging Shopify’s platform, your first identity has to be an entrepreneur.
Entrepreneurship requires not just one skillset, but an amalgamation of disparate and
eclectic skillets that makes it so hard to be successful. If you are just good at making
something and not so good at all the other things, you are much better off building a
busines in a marketplace than on top of a platform. Shopify does not really help much
in generating demand for its merchants, it is the merchants’ job to drive demand. Of
course, sometimes your product itself can drive demand through superior/unique
quality, but more often than not, sellers prefer a helping hand in driving demand. I
admit the businesses that are built in marketplaces are not as resilient, but you are far
more likely to put food on your table *today*
. My guess is we are hardwired to take the
trade-off to have food on our table today vs some probability event of losing it in the
future when marketplace businesses make arbitrary changes or even worse, if we get
kicked out of the marketplace for some reason. Moreover, it’s not like there aren’t wild
success stories out there of people who built massive businesses entirely on
marketplaces. Anker, an Amazon native brand and founded by ex-Google engineer
Steven Yang, went public in Shenzen Stock Exchange in Aug 2020. Its LTM sales is
~$1.5 Bn with a market cap of ~$9 Bn.
Many sellers (and investors) complain about the “exorbitant” fees marketplace
businesses charge. Marketplace businesses can afford to impose these fees because
they know exactly how much value they can bring to your business. I read a very
interesting piece written by an Amazon seller. He sells educational toys online and did
$7.4 mn sales in 2020 across different marketplaces/channels. Despite his earnest

=== PAGE 18 ===
effort to diversify, he still sells 93% of the products in Amazon. His attempt to diversify
is quite intuitive, especially when you see Amazon had a whopping ~52% take-rate
whereas a sale generated in his own website just cost him ~17% of the revenues. There
are lots of interesting, nuanced observation in that piece, but this one particularly
stood out to me:
“$4.6 million dollars worth of product were added to carts on our website in 2020. We
finished the year with $97,000 in sales. The conversion rate on our website was 0.7% in
2020. The conversion rate for a typical Amazon item for us is about 40%.”
The trust Amazon (and other marketplaces) built over decades is just extremely
difficult to replicate by a random website on the internet. As majority of US consumers
start their product search directly on Amazon, it is hard to abdicate marketplaces for a
seller. When you add the ability to reach all over the US in just two days (increasingly
one day going forward), you realize there is a very steep trade-off sellers may have to
make if they just focus on eye-popping margin/profitability instead of eye-popping
topline with reasonable margin.
￼
Some might wonder that a merchant does not have to make these trade-offs as they
can just focus on multiple channels. Alex Danco made the same point:
“The first thing to note is that Amazon, Etsy, and Walmart are all channels of Shopify,
right? So it's like we compete with them in one sense, but also not really because you
can sell on Etsy from your Shopify store, you can sell on Amazon from your Shopify
store. You can even sell on Walmart through a Shopify store. You can't actually add up

=== PAGE 19 ===
these numbers and get a total pie and then divide that pie and attribute which GMV is
going to where, because in some places, they overlap. There is a lot of Amazon sales
that happens that ultimately feeds back to a Shopify store.
”
One investor I spoke with about Shopify mentioned he is less concerned about GMV,
but more concerned about take rates, especially in a potential situation if marketplaces
trump the DTC channel. As Danco pointed out, GMVs can be double counted if you use
Shopify platform but sell things in a marketplace. While both marketplace and Shopify
can count it as GMV, Shopify’s take rates can potentially be lot lower if the merchant
sells on marketplace instead of on his/her own website. Therefore, while success of
marketplaces is not existential question for Shopify, they would much prefer their
merchants be able to sell on their Shopify store.
There is, however, no denying that SMBs were at the forefront on the DTC revolution in
the last 10-12 years for which Shopify deserves a lot of credit. One McKinsey report
mentioned that large CPG brands contributed just 25% of the industry growth despite
generating half of the overall sales in the last four years. Many of these large CPG
brands are now under pressure and giving it a serious thought to replicate the SMBs’
success by going DTC.

=== PAGE 20 ===
￼
Many of these large CPG brands may end up building DTC, but their success or failure
will hardly depend on efficacy of Shopify’s platform. While Shopify certainly eliminates
the friction out of the decision to go the DTC route, it cannot help you succeed in
aggregating demand. The same McKinsey report indicated DTC is “commercially viable
for only select CPG propositions—namely, those with an average basket and purchase
frequency high enough to justify customer acquisition costs and make per-order
economics viable.
”
As someone who hasn’t spent much time studying CPG brands, I am not quite
comfortable in sharing my opinion on their potential success (or failure). I was
reminded of this sense of discomfort after reading the following from Benedict Evans:
“
…half of the Shopify story is actually big companies - Heinz, or Unilever. Why are they

How about the SMBs or startups? Can the DTC revolution, powered by Shopify, gain
even more momentum in the next 10-15 years? In many ways, the last decade or so
was an anomaly in the CPG space. From 1923 to 1983, the top brands in more than 10
categories were unchanged. Large CPG companies enjoyed the largest competitive
advantage duration compared to probably any sector out there. Despite all the success
of DTC brands in the last decade or so, I do wonder whether the best days of DTC are
behind us. Some of my concerns are mentioned in this HBR piece on DTC published
last year:
“The direct-to-consumer startups’ rise was enabled by an environment of abundant
venture capital, low competition, and above all, the advertising arbitrage that could be
exploited on under-priced social media platforms.
Fast forward to today, and many of those businesses are looking less viable than they
once were.
The landscape is almost unrecognizable from even a decade ago (save for the sans-
serif fonts). For one, competitors have flooded in, spurred by the early success of a few
standout brands. This has driven up the price of social media ads, eliminating any
arbitrage and making the customer acquisition math that much more challenging.

Now-established players have learned that Instagram advertisements and influencer

I do sense a potentially large market for Shopify in the subscale brick & mortar retailers
who mostly have terrible websites and can use help from Shopify’s cutting-edge e-

& mortar retailers who will choose Shopify in the near future. I have a hard time figuring
out how big this opportunity can be.
But overall, I believe marketplaces will turn out to be superior model and most DTCs will
probably find out how “unfair” the fight is. Of course, some will build engaging

” The bar is clearly

marketplace business model, it is a natural question that I and most Shopify investors

Should Shopify build a marketplace?
There are two school of thoughts on this question: one school thinks it is a natural
extension of Shopify’s business model and very much attuned with “arming the rebels”
.
Web Smith from 2PM is on this camp. The other school believes building a marketplace
is deeply antagonistic to the core value proposition of Shopify. Stratechery’s Ben
Thompson and NZS Capital’s Brad Slingerlend seem to be on this boat. Let’s first hear
them out before I share what I think.
Build the marketplace
Excerpt from Web Smith piece:
“Shopify has armed the rebels by supplying some of them with the funds necessary to
operate or expand. Now, it needs to influence the demand curve for the businesses on
its platforms. Shopify needs to become an evangelist for its brands.
Given the market position that Shopify has earned, it’s become clear that Lütke’s
position on psychological support must change and it should have begun with Super
Bowl LIV. Shopify’s promotional power could reduce insurgent competition while closing

=== PAGE 24 ===
the gap with the incumbent company that it is challenging: Amazon. Shopify must
evolve into its own marketplace. As customer acquisition costs rise for small-to-middle
market retailers, Amazon has become a reasonable partner for retailers looking to
increase top-of-funnel awareness.
Lütke’s likely opposition to this idea is clear: By selecting brands or products to feature
in a marketplace format, Shopify becomes a kingmaker of sorts. A kingmaker is a
person or organization with great influence on the value of a candidate. This person or
organization uses policy, finance, and competitive forces to influence succession. I
contend that offering loans or advancements to merchants is another form of
kingmaking. Now that Shopify has begun to market financial products, there is less of
an argument to be made.
…direct-to-consumer brands were noticeably absent, priced out by the exorbitant
costs of doing business. Imagine a $5.7 million, 30-second advertisement that sent
tens of millions of Americans to marketplace.shopify.com. When those potential
customers, developers, and consumers arrive: they’d see a curation of Shopify’s
greatest brands – new and old, established and fresh. Shopify wouldn’t have only
gained new customers or partnership prospects. Shopify would have influenced the
awareness, growth, and viability for a number of brands that are dependent on three
key resources.
When you arm the rebels, do whatever you can to make sure that they win. They’re
fighting for their supplier as much as they’re fighting for their own well-being. After all,
their war is now your war.

Don’t build the marketplace
From Brad Slingerlend’s piece:
“Ecommerce marketplaces always have a tension between serving the consumer and
serving the merchant. Both constituents are customers of the company that runs the
marketplace, but they have competing needs – the customer isn’t always right, but a
marketplace has to assume they are at the expense of the seller. Meg Whitman buried
eBay’s ecommerce lead in the mid-2000s by completely fumbling this delicate balance
between consumer and merchant, which allowed Amazon to build a huge platform of
3rd-party sellers thanks to their obsessive focus on customer satisfaction. The rapidly

=== PAGE 25 ===
growing ecommerce platform Shopify could face a similar tension in its business model
in the next 5-10 years. The company has been successful in large part by focusing on
the merchants, and it does not (yet) run a customer-focused marketplace.
If Shopify chose to run a full marketplace, it would face the same set of issues that
stalled eBay’s growth rate. I used to count myself among the many investors who want
Shopify to run a centralized marketplace, but I’ve since evolved my view. A marketplace
would require a consumer focus at odds with Shopify’s maniacal focus on serving
entrepreneurs. There are, however, products where you can solve for both the buyer
and the seller. Shopify’s payment engine is one such example – Shop Pay is both a very
good consumer experience and improves sales for merchants. Shopify’s fulfillment
ambitions may be another dual-sided solution without the need of a full marketplace.
Shopify is an enabler of direct relationships between buyer and seller without a
marketplace in between, and it charges a toll on transactions that is far smaller than
what Amazon or other retailers impose. In return for that lower toll, merchants often
need to spend marketing dollars online if their product doesn’t sell itself. Merchants on
Shopify’s platform are in a survival-of-the-fittest landscape – if they can meet the
needs of their customers, they will thrive, and, if they cannot, there is no centralized
platform to prop them up via ads and a large user base; likewise, there is no middleman
to suppress them in marketplace search results or charge a large tax on sales.
”
Ben Thompson had a largely similar point: “…suggesting that Shopify take responsibility
for directly acquiring customers (as opposed to building tools to help merchants do so)
is not only out of step with Shopify’s position in the value chain, but there is also no
particularly good reason to believe that Shopify will be any better at this than their
merchants.
”

Personally, I think one of the reasons Amazon has been so incredibly successful is
Amazon literally tried everything that you can possibly try in retail without making any
philosophical assumption of what it should or should not do. If you think about 1P and
3P dynamics and how it bamboozles regulators (although it is hardly a novel idea in
retail, Walmart/Target/Costco have been doing it for decades; Amazon is just more

marketplace. If Amazon listened to such suggestion, it could end up making Amazon a
laggard to eBay which actually had higher market cap than Amazon in most of 2000-
2010 period. In fact, Amazon even tried its hands on building a Shopify-like platform in
2006: Webstore. They decided to close Webstore in 2015, and it is funny that Amazon
actually referred its clients to switch to Shopify. With Whole Foods acquisition and
Amazon Go, Amazon has its physical footprint in the retail market as well. Amazon
doesn’t have any fascination with a particular narrative, it just doubles down on
whatever works, and if it doesn’t seem to work, they move on.

brand name of the store, and not by products. If your store’s name is “Mr. Beast” and
you sell shoes, you will not rank higher (or may not even appear at all) in the search

Section 3: Shopify’s path (and resistance) to
dominate the global retail operating system
Shopify’s ambition to be the operating system of global retail has weak competition
from its direct competitors, and almost all of the roadblocks ahead of Shopify to realize
its ambition will likely come from players (e.g. Facebook) in the broader ecosystem
many of whom may not appear as direct competitors today. In this section, I will
discuss three things: competitive dynamics in the status quo SMB e-commerce market,
competition for ancillary products/services, and the Facebook threat.
Shopify way ahead of the curve in the status quo SMB e-commerce platform
business
Shopify estimates the total number of SMBs (<500 employees) globally to be 47
million. “The 10th man” used BuiltWith data to estimate just 9% (excluding China) of e-
commerce websites utilized one of the seven biggest non-Chinese platforms (Shopify,
WooCommerce, Wix, Magento, Weebly, Squarespace, BigCommerce) to build a
website. The number of SMBs grew at ~60 bps/year and it is likely that 50-70% of
these SMBs will need to have a functioning website by the next 10-15 years which
implies the potential subscribers for the overall industry can be 5-7x from here during
the same timeframe. Of course, it’s not just SMBs but there are also tens of thousands
of enterprise businesses as well who are in the target market as well, especially for
products such as Shopify Plus. While from unit perspective, they can be dwarfed by

=== PAGE 30 ===
the broader SMB category, they have a substantial impact from volume or GMV
perspective. Shopify currently has ~20% market share in the overall e-commerce
platform business.
One consistent theme among current crop of direct competitors is almost nobody has
the similar breadth of products/services as Shopify does. The status quo competitive
dynamics is adequately explained by “The 10th man” in the following two paragraphs:
“We spent some time looking at each of Shopify’s competitors, and one thing stood out
to us immediately: few competitors, if any, are “merchant obsessed”
. Most competitors,
including Wix, Squarespace, Weebly, WooCommerce, and Magento, are either part of
larger entities with other products to focus on, or aren’t exclusively focused on e-
commerce: the majority of Wix, Squarespace, and Weebly subscribers seem to be
outside of e-commerce; WooCommerce is actually just a plug-in for WordPress
websites, which makes onboarding awkward; and Magento is owned by Adobe, but
has changed hands multiple times in the last decade, and only focuses on enterprise
customers. Shopify, on the other hand, has been a standalone entity since 2004, and
only focuses on one thing: e-commerce. As a result, Shopify seems to have a better
product, with faster updates, a simpler onboarding process, and the widest offering of
solutions.
In lots of cases, competitors don’t even target the entire market. WooCommerce
doesn’t have an enterprise product, and Magento doesn’t have an SMB product. The
team at WooCommerce won’t be thinking as intimately about the commerce problems
that enterprises face, and the team at Magento won’t be thinking as intimately about
the problems SMBs face. Without the holistic view on product development, and the
same economies of scale benefits on R&D and marketing, it will be hard for
WooCommerce and Magento to effectively compete with Shopify.
”
What makes things even more in Shopify’s favor is its own e-commerce app
marketplace that lets third-party developers reach Shopify’s merchants with their apps.
I think we all intuitively understand how powerful the flywheel of an “Appstore” can be,
and Shopify can further differentiate itself from the core commodity business by
helping merchants utilize lots of tools/apps in their business.
However, by now, we also understand the risk that is implied in building your product
on top of someone else’s Appstore. The question remains how Shopify will think about

=== PAGE 31 ===
its ecosystem as they continue to scale the business. We have already seen some
tension within the Shopify Appstore when Mailchimp was delisted from the Appstore.
This sub-reddit did a good job of covering both sides. Platform risk is pretty real,
especially when Shopify can build and prioritize its own apps vs the direct competitors
which can improve its own take rate.
While the apps built on Shopify AppStore get distribution thanks to Shopify’s reach to
merchants, investors probably do take platform risk into account. Many successful
apps within Shopify Appstore which grew 2-3x faster than Shopify itself and yet
received valuation of 10x ARR which is 25-30% of the valuation multiple Shopify itself
has been trading at. If such discrepancy in valuation persists and platform risk
becomes increasingly real, this can pose a dent to Shopify’s larger ambition with its
Appstore. At the moment, thanks to the high-octane growth in e-commerce/DTC,
developers have largely ignored this risk, but that can change if high-profile fallouts
(e.g. Mailchimp) start happening more frequently.
Competition for ancillary products/services
Shopify’s ambition to be the global retail operating system has two blind spots: it
cannot help merchants generate demand, and it does not have a robust logistics
solution. While the former is somewhat incongruent with the business model itself,
logistics is a very critical component in the e-commerce business if you want to build
an extremely large resilient businesses, especially when your long-term competition is
Amazon. If Shopify can build a robust end-to-end native logistics solution, I think that
will prove to be game over for its direct competitors (Magento, BigCommerce,
WooCommerce of the world). In such a scenario, Shopify will probably enjoy an
outsized share of e-commerce platform business and indeed can truly become the
global retail operating system.
The first question that comes to mind is whether Shopify will focus on partnering with
third-party logistics (3PL) companies or building an Amazon-like fulfilment network
itself. The base case is they will focus on partnership in the initial years but will ramp
up building their own fulfilment network in future. If it’s just partnership with 3PL
companies, its direct competitors can also do similar deals with the same 3PL
companies and therefore, it may not be a strong differentiator. But no other direct
competitor of Shopify has even a remote possibility of thinking about building their own
fulfilment network with warehouses and delivery logistics.

=== PAGE 32 ===
Although I am no logistics expert, it does seem to me that the path to get there can
prove to be extremely difficult for Shopify. Logistics is extremely capital-intensive
business and the economics may not be very lucrative even though it can certainly
enhance moats of Shopify. For example, the cumulative capex of Fedex from 2005 to
2019 was $55 Bn, and yet the total Enterprise Value of the company was ~$60 Bn at
the end of 2019. Thanks to the pandemic, the stock has soared in 2020 and now its EV
is ~$105 Bn.
Let’s look at Amazon now. In 2011, Amazon had ~44 mn sqft fulfilment capacity. Its
capacity has increased ~10x over the last 10 years to reach 402 mn sqft in 2020.
Please note data centers’ capacity (AWS) was ~18 mn sqft in 2020, so ~95% of
Amazon’s 402 mn sqft capacity is used for its e-commerce fulfilment. This excellent
video on Amazon logistics can give you an idea how massive and complex Amazon’s
logistics operation is.
As you can see below, for every dollar of GMV, it required to have ~0.8 sqft capacity. If
in 10 years Shopify drives ~$1 trillion GMV on behalf of its merchants and 15% of the
GMV is part of its own fulfillment network, it will require to build ~150 mn sqft capacity.
Of course, Amazon is not going to stop expanding its own capacity anytime soon. In 10
years, they will probably surpass 1 billion sqft in capacity. One concern I have for
Shopify’s fulfilment ambition is what if Amazon continues to expand at a rapid pace and
start offering its logistics service in white label to Shopify’s merchants. I believe it is in
Amazon’s best interest to not let Shopify build its own fulfilment network. Amazon has
Prime and AWS to throw gargantuan amount of cash every year which they can invest
heavily to build this while label logistics solution. If they undercut Shopify’s logistics
pricing by 10-20%, I wonder how many merchants will opt for Amazon’s solution
instead. I don’t know the answer to this, but it is something that crossed my mind.

The Facebook threat
Facebook or social media in general can pose threat to platforms such as Shopify in
multiple ways. Social e-commerce, especially livestreaming e-commerce is increasingly
gaining momentum in international markets. Sales from livestreaming e-commerce
doubled to reach $60 Bn in 2020. I have a personal experience that helped me
understand the power of livestreaming e-commerce.
My elder brother and his wife live in Bangladesh. My sister-in-law graduated from
college five years ago but was struggling to get a decent job. She worked for a few
months at a place that paid her $250-300/month (not horrible in Bangladesh’s
standard, but yes, it wasn’t good money). She eventually left her job and almost on a
whim opened a Facebook page “Passion For Fashion” (PFF) to sell women’s apparel
online. She started doing livestream, and it just took off very fast. After doing this for a
year on her own, my brother realized this is getting too much for her to manage on her
own. Then my brother left his job to focus on this.

=== PAGE 35 ===
This is how PFF works: They source products from wholesalers in Bangladesh or import
the products from India/Pakistan. She shows the products during the livestreaming on
Facebook, and customers just take screenshot of the products they like and send it via
Facebook Messenger. Then an employee from PFF would respond to that and if they
have the product/size in stock, customer would send his/her address. PFF partnered
with a courier service to send the product and collect cash (card penetration is <5% in
Bangladesh, some also transfer money in bKash which is Bangladesh’s most popular
mobile wallet).
Every time she livestreams (usually 5/6 times a week for an hour per day), it currently
leads to $2-3K sales per livestream. The business has ~30% operating margin and ~10
employees. Recently, PFF also launched its first physical outlet which enhanced the
credibility of the Facebook page even more as my brother told me number of viewers
and yield from each livestreaming increased following the launch of the physical outlet.
How much does Facebook take from this business? Almost nothing. My brother just
spends $200-300 per month (i.e. 0.005% take rate for Facebook) advertising the page
on Facebook. PFF doesn’t even have a website yet. They do plan on building one and I
did suggest them to take a look at Shopify.
But the success of PFF alludes to the susceptibility of Shopify if livestreaming e-
commerce becomes a larger share of total e-commerce. I am sure thousands of
influencers around the world will try to make this work and while some will probably be
successful in transitioning their audience from social media to their own website, the
supermajority will be stuck within social network such as Facebook/Instagram/other
social media.
It is important to remember that Facebook Shops/Instagram Shopping are still at its
nascent stage and by no means, their success is guaranteed. But they do seem well
positioned at the moment. Shopify merchants depend on social media ads for traffic to
their website. If they focus building their presence on social media, especially since
Facebook just makes money on ads and has no monetization plan to take a cut as a
marketplace does, Shopify’s significance on the broader ecosystem gets incrementally
diminished. Ben Thompson elaborated on this point:

=== PAGE 36 ===
Shopify’s merchants, however, are hugely dependent on advertising, particularly on
Facebook, but given that Shopify and Facebook are different companies, that meant
that one or both had to work with 3rd-party data (i.e. Facebook with Shopify data) for
advertising to be effective. By cutting that off Apple is forcing a shift in the point of
integration in the e-commerce value chain: Facebook will now integrate advertising
and a Facebook-based storefront, which will allow it to measure how effectively its ads
convert. Shopify, meanwhile, can still provide payments, but it will need to invest ever
more heavily in other merchant services if it is going to preserve its own integration in
the value chain, or it risks becoming a commoditized player.
This shift in Shopify’s business model is almost certainly why the company appears to
be uncomfortable with this evolution of their “partnership” with Facebook. Sure,
Facebook Shop integration will be a feature of Shopify’s Subscription Solutions, but
Shopify will be locked out of sales made via Facebook Checkout, which means no
Merchant Solutions revenue, and by extension, no participating in the upside of its
merchants’ growth.
Facebook Checkout, on the other hand, would mean a purchase that is completed in
seconds, not minutes. After all, if anyone knows who you are, it is Facebook! That is
going to mean a lot more conversions on Facebook and Instagram ads in particular —
and Shopify isn’t going to see an incremental cent.
Remember that a whole bunch of those Instagram advertisements are from Shopify
merchants, all of whose websites are hosted on the same infrastructure. Image if
Shopify could set a cookie when a user made a purchase on one website, and then
when the user visited another Shopify website they were already logged in with all of
their payment information ready to go. Notably, Shopify has built this infrastructure —
it’s called Shop Pay — but you have to log in on every distinct Shopify website.
The Facebook threat is also why it makes enormous sense for Shopify to build its own
fulfilment network even if it comes at the expense of lower margins. As discussed
earlier, the path to success is going to be difficult because of the capital intensity and
potential strong response from Amazon, but if they are successful, it will ensure and
possibly enhance their value to the broader e-commerce ecosystem. From the same
Ben Thompson piece:

=== PAGE 37 ===
“This, though, is why I remain so excited about the Shopify Fulfillment Network. I’m not
completely sold on Shopify’s approach — I thought it might make more sense to try and
create a common interface for merchants to interact with independent 3PL providers,
as opposed to building out Shopify own logistics service (but I could very well be
wrong about this) — but I absolutely endorse the company making massive
investments in this space.
First, this is a service that no merchant can build on its own; it is a perfect example of
how a platform can create something for an ecosystem that would not exist otherwise.
Moreover, this is specifically what is needed to fulfill the promise I laid out last year, of
Shopify as an Amazon competitor, not because users choose to go to Shopify, but
because they have no reason to know that Shopify exists.
Second, this is a service that Facebook Shops is going to make more valuable, not less:
all of those merchants increasing sales thanks to Facebook Checkout still need to ship
their things, and there is zero chance that Facebook ever integrates into the real world.
Third, this is a service no one else is going to build. Yes, it is very difficult to build
fulfillment centers and develop robots and employ lots of workers, but within that
difficulty is an escape from competition, i.e. a far more reliable way to make sustainable
profits in the long run.
”
Section 5: Valuation/model assumptions
As always, if you are reading my deep dive for the first time, I encourage you to read
my approach to valuation first. I follow an expectation investing or reverse DCF
approach and try to derive what is embedded in the current stock price.
Even for regular readers, there are some changes that I would like to highlight in this
month’s deep dive. As mentioned earlier, I really loved the way “The 10th man”
approached Shopify’s model. His model was publicly available, and we spoke for two
hours going through his current thoughts on Shopify as well as some questions that I
had on his model. In the end, I became so influenced by his model that when I started
building one, it was becoming a clone of his model. I finally decided to just use his
model and give credit to him. I updated his model with 2020 numbers and made many

=== PAGE 38 ===
changes in assumptions. While 10th man and I are both somewhat ambivalent on
Shopify as a stock on current prices, he is somewhat more bullish than I am.
Two things I would like mention before I delve into the model. First, this model can
seem slightly more complex than my ones if you are a beginner in excel/financial
modeling. And second, when I was going through Shopify model, I was reminded of the
St. Petersburg Paradox and the resemblance the paradox may have for high growth
stocks such as Shopify. I wrote this piece on this topic six months ago. Shopify truly
fits this paradox. It is somewhat impossible to come up with any point estimate or even
a low range for the stock price with any reasonable confidence. The range of outcome
is pretty wide and spreadsheet-based model on Shopify is just not that useful. While
the model may not be useful, it helps me (and you) visualize the business in more
granular level and helps me connect the dots quicker. I will briefly discuss a back-of-
the-envelope type valuation of Shopify after I discuss the model.
As mentioned in Section 1, Shopify reports its revenues in two segments: Subscription
Solutions and Merchant Solutions. I will discuss each of these segments separately.
Subscription Solutions
Shopify generates almost all of its subscription revenue from its four core subscription
plans: Basic Shopify ($29/month), Shopify ($79/month), Advanced Shopify
($299/month), and Shopify Plus ($2,000/month or 0.25% of GMV with max fee being
$40,000/month) While Shopify discloses total number of merchants on its platform, it
does not break it down further by each of these plans. But it is highly likely that
supermajority of the merchants is in the basic plan and only a tiny minority opt for
standard and advanced plan. Shopify, however, does disclose the number of Shopify
Plus merchants.
While it may seem 18% growth in merchants next year as too low compared to
historical numbers, we should take into account of the impact of pandemic. In fact,
there is a reasonable chance that even this may prove to be optimistic. For example,
recent BuiltWith data shows that number of live Shopify websites has gone down from
1.7 mn a month ago to 1.4 mn now. As more and more businesses fail that was
launched during the pandemic, number of merchants growth may slow down
considerably.

=== PAGE 39 ===
In terms of pricing, Shopify has not changed its prices apart from one increase in price
for Shopify Advanced from $179/month to $299/month in 2016. “The 10th man”
assumed 5% price increases for all the plans in 2025 and 2030 which seems
reasonable to me. Having said that, international markets will be of paramount
importance for Shopify’s growth story going forward. While $79/month may be pretty
cheap for a merchant in the US, it may not be deemed cheap by an Indian merchant. If
Shopify wants to penetrate every little corner of the world except China, it may need to
maintain its current pricing much longer.
￼
Merchant Solutions
Merchant solutions can be segmented into three categories: payments,
shipping/fulfilment, and Shopify Capital/Balance. Let me discuss each of these
separately. Please note that I have assumed zero revenue from the Shop app. If
Shopify indeed builds a (quasi)marketplace in its rebranded Shop app, the model
needs to be updated.
￼

=== PAGE 40 ===
Payments: While ~32% of GMV was transacted via Shop Pay in 2015, it has
consistently increased 200-300 bps each year to reach ~45% in 2020. As Shop Pay
becomes available in more and more countries, penetration may increase to ~60% in
the long-term. As Shopify merchants use multiple channels (Facebook, Instagram,
Walmart etc.) to sell their products, there may be some ceiling on how deep the
penetration can be in the long run. The pace of Shop Pay integration in international
markets may play an important role here. Shopify’s recent Shopify POS Pro launch
means as more and more merchants/retailers focus on omnichannel capabilities, Shop
Pay will enjoy a growing share of the merchant wallet.
The other thing that is hard to model and difficult to forecast is how Shopify’s reach of
SMB merchants will lead to potential opportunities in the future that is not currently not
quite visible. For example, Affirm made a deal with Shopify to be its exclusive “Buy Now
Pay Later” (BNPL) partner. For that privilege, Shopify was granted warrants to buy up
to 20.3 million shares (8% stake) in Affirm. Just 6 months after the deal, Affirm came to
IPO and Shopify’s stake is worth ~$2 Bn.
￼
Shopify Shipping and SFN: It is estimated that almost two-fifth GMV adopted Shopify
Shipping in 2020. But as Shopify focuses on building its fulfilment network, most
shipping customers will just shift to Shopify Fulfilment Network (SFN). Within SFN,
Shopify is likely to pursue both approaches: at one hand, they are partnering with 3PL
companies, and on the other hand, they will also likely build end-to-end fulfilment
capacity. As you can see below, while I expect ~5% of total GMV to be utilized in
Shopify-owned SFN, it will likely be scaled to reach ~40% by 2030. Let me walk you
through some calculation here.

=== PAGE 41 ===
As discussed earlier, sqft per $1 Bn GMV hovered between 0.77 to 0.85 in the last few
years for Amazon. While 10thman initially assumed 0.65 sqft/$1 Bn GMV, we both later
agreed that this is probably too low, especially after looking at Amazon. I wanted to
assume the higher end of Amazon’s historical benchmark for a few reasons: First,
Amazon has been investing in logistics for two decades and their level of efficiency
may not match with Shopify given lack of experience and learning curve that’s perhaps
required to ramp up; Second, I believe Shopify, even after further along the learning
curve, may be lot less efficient than Amazon since the packages for each merchant are
all different and inventory turnover likely to be lot lower than Amazon.
Although 10thman assumed $65 cost per square foot, he later let me know that he
probably undershot on the costs as well since he did not take into account the cost of
land and therefore in reality, it maybe ~$90-100 per square foot.
Take-rate for SFN in-source fulfilment was assumed to be 12.5%. Amazon mentioned in
2018 10-k that their overall 3P take-rate was 26.7%. If we assume marketplace take-
rate as 15%, FBA take rate is 12-15% (depending on the mix between FBA and FBM
within Amazon marketplace). Fulfilment for 3PL partnership model was assumed to be
0.5%. It is possible that I am being conservative here, but I have no clarity of the
potential take rate here. It may well be 1% as Shopify is likely to sell/rent 6RS robots to
partner network. By 2030, it is assumed in-source SFN will reach half of the total (in-
source and partnership) SFN mix.
In terms of margin, gross margin for SFN partnership model was assumed to be 55% in
2021 and gradually increased to 75% once the partnership model scales further. On the
other hand, gross margin for in-source SFN was assumed 27.5% and kept at this level
for the forecasting period. This is in-line with FedEx’s gross margin, so while it may
seem reasonable for out years, it is possible I am being generous on the in-source
gross margin in the initial years. As internal SFN model ramps up in out years, its
revenue contribution increases significantly and gross margin consequently starts
going down. Shopify Shipping is assumed to generate 36% gross margin, but given
SFN’s scaled operations, its contribution will be insignificant in out years.

=== PAGE 42 ===
￼
Shopify Capital/Balance: As described in the beginning, Shopify Capital is a loan
product whereas Balance is merchant banking account. 10thman looked into Aswath
Damodaran’s working capital dataset which is typically 13-14% of total sales. If we
assume Shopify Balance as effectively the source of the fund financing the loans,
Shopify will essentially earn a spread between these products which is assumed to be
~3.5%. While ancillary services such as this further differentiates Shopify from
competing commoditized alternatives and enhance its value propositions, Shopify
Capital/Balance may not move the needle much.
￼
Profitability:

=== PAGE 43 ===
Shopify is all about the end-state. Profitability is largely masked because of significant
investment through income statement (higher S&M and R&D), but long-term EBIT
margins are assumed to reach ~20% in 2030. There are too many moving parts here as
you can probably expect.
￼
One thing I would like to highlight here is the below table. Notice how the revenue
composition has changed from 2015 to 2020 and then notice again how the revenue
mix is assumed to change in 2030. While Shopify used to generate majority of its
revenue from subscription solution (i.e. SMB merchants running/managing e-commerce
websites), thanks to its successful payments integration/adoption to its platform, in
just five years Shopify generated majority of its revenue from payments. As less than
half of GMV currently utilizes Shop Pay, it will continue to dominate overall revenue mix
for the next few years until SFN will start ramping up and reach some scale. Basically,
half of Shopify’s 2030 revenue is expected to come from a segment they just launched.
￼
I worry about market’s implied assumption that SFN is destined to succeed. It is hard to
blame Mr. Market given the quality of management and their history of topnotch
execution. But if FedEx and Amazon are any indication, logistics just seems like a
different beast altogether. For example, operating margin for FedEx never reached
~10% in the last 15 years, and the average operating margin during this time was
~7.5%. Since SFN consists both partnership model and in-source fulfilment, it is not an
apple-to-apple comparison with FedEx. But let me unpack a little why ~20% EBIT
margin may still not be too difficult for Shopify to achieve. If SFN in-source model
generates ~5% EBIT margin in 2030, SFN partnership model needs to generate ~25%
EBIT margin, and the rest of Shopify needs to generate 25% EBIT margin for the overall

EBIT margin to reach 20%. Those don’t seem particularly lofty number to hit for
Shopify.

is simply jaw dropping and from my conversations with several investors/analysts, I
could sense very few people will be surprised if Shopify proves my cautious tone as
just “analysis paralysis”
