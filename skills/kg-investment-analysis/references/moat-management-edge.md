## 1. Moat: 7 Powers, trend, profit pool, customer

A Power is a condition that lets a company earn persistent returns above its competitors (Helmer, *7 Powers*). Each one pairs a **benefit** with a **barrier**. Name at most two Powers per company; if you need three, you are probably counting brand plus habit twice.

| Power | Benefit | Barrier | Test that it is real |
|---|---|---|---|
| Scale economies | Unit cost falls with volume | A challenger must accept losses to reach scale | Cost per unit falls with size; the largest player has the category's best margin |
| Network economies | Each user makes the product worth more to the others | A challenger must build liquidity on both sides at once | Take rate holds while volume grows; little multi-homing |
| Counter-positioning | A new model the incumbent can't copy without hurting itself | The incumbent's rational refusal | The incumbent visibly declines to copy; the challenger's margins rise as it scales |
| Switching costs | The customer loses value by leaving | Embedded data, workflow, training, integration | Retention and NRR above 100%; price increases stick |
| Branding | Higher willingness to pay for an objectively similar product | Decades of consistent signals | A sustained price premium with stable or rising volume share |
| Cornered resource | Preferential access to an asset others can't get | Patent, license, location, a team, an allocation | The asset can be named and dated, with a known expiry |
| Process power | Embedded routines that lower cost or raise quality | Years to build; opaque from outside | The margin or throughput edge persists after rivals copy the visible product |

Common mislabels:
- Reach without a cost advantage is not scale.
- A loyalty program without a price premium is not branding.
- First-mover status without embedded data is not a switching cost.

**The §3 moat table has four rows:**

| Row | What it says |
|---|---|
| Mechanism | The Powers, with evidence. |
| Trend | Widening, stable or narrowing, plus the number that decides it. Weigh the evidence in this order: pricing power (the last increase and what volume did) → share → retention or churn → gross margin against the closest peer → rivals adding or removing capacity. |
| Profit pool | Who in the value chain captures the margin, and which way that is moving: a compressed version of the five forces. |
| Customer | Who buys and what job the product does · retention or repeat (or a disclosed proxy) · the last price increase and the volume response · CAC and LTV where disclosed. A customer business is worth the sum of its cohorts. Rising retention with stable CAC compounds; falling retention with rising CAC is a business buying its growth. |

Then give the rating in one sentence: **Wide / Narrow / None**, plus the trend.

## 2. Scale economies shared

This applies to any company whose Power is Scale. The Trend row states whether **scale gains go to price or to margin** (Nomad Investment Partnership; Costco and Amazon are the archetypes).

- **Shared.** Prices are cut, or held flat against inflation, and the savings come from productivity. The evidence: gross margin steady or falling while operating cost per unit falls, price falling relative to rivals, and customer frequency or share rising. Each turn of the loop widens the moat, and a business that isn't over-earning is hard to attack.
- **Harvested.** Margins expand on the back of price increases. The moat is being spent, and the high margin invites entry.

## 3. Competition, scuttlebutt, TAM and the big-market check

- **Competitor table.** List the direct competitors with share where known, and a one-line threat read for each. Then say how this company differs, with evidence. A structurally unique business gets one line instead of a table.
- **Scuttlebutt** (Fisher). Read what competitors, suppliers and customers disclose about this company and its category:
  - competitors' latest results and calls;
  - 10%-customer disclosures in suppliers' 10-Ks;
  - dated pricing pages.

  Tag anything that is not primary. One good competitor read often decides the Trend row.
- **TAM and industry structure.** Size the market and name the structure: fragmented, consolidated or winner-take-most.
- **Big-market check** (Cornell & Damodaran, 2020). When several listed contenders are priced for leadership in the same large market, sum the revenue their prices or Base cases imply at year five and compare that total with a plausible TAM. Collective overpricing can exist even when each story is plausible on its own.

## 4. Management: incentives, guidance grade, promises ledger

- **Incentives.** One line on what the CEO is paid for: the metrics and weights from the DEF 14A's CD&A. Two questions:
  - Does it pay for per-share value (ROIC, FCF per share, relative TSR) or for size (revenue, adjusted EPS)?
  - What share of pay is long-term equity?
- **Guidance grade** over the last 8–12 quarters, plus the one number that decides it:
  - `Consistent beater`;
  - `Mixed`: often beats the quarter but misses the year;
  - `Misser`.
- **Promises ledger.** Three rows: what letters or investor days committed to three to five years ago, and what actually happened (🟢 / 🟡 / 🔴).
- **CEO/CFO read.** Tenure, background, and any governance flags: dual-class shares, restatements, an activist settlement.

## 5. Capital allocation: Outsider grade and the $1 retention test

- **Outsider grade** (Thorndike, *The Outsiders*). One line plus one sentence of evidence, anchored on **countercyclical buyback discipline**: does the company buy more when the stock is cheap and go quiet when it is dear? Other inputs are a per-share cash-flow mindset, disciplined M&A, and independence from the herd.

  Vocabulary: `Outsider · Outsider-leaning · Reinvestor · Steward (not Outsider) · Anti-Outsider`. A Dividend King is usually a Steward.
- **The $1 retention test** (Buffett, *Owner's Manual*, principle 9). Does retained capital create at least $1 of market value per $1 retained, over five years?

  **Market value per $1 retained = Δ market cap (5 years) ÷ Σ (net income − dividends − net buybacks)**

  - Equity issuance counts as negative buybacks.
  - If the denominator is negative, write `n/m`.
  - Read the result against the S&P 500's change over the same window. Buffett himself corrected the test because a falling market can fail a good allocator.
  - When dividends and buybacks exceed earnings, a debt-funded program can hide behind a small denominator, so show the change in net debt next to it.
- **Also report:**
  - dividends + buybacks as % of owner earnings;
  - M&A spend and the ROIIC of each deal where it can be isolated;
  - buyback timing against the price.

## 6. Appearances and letters

- **Letters.** Five fiscal years: standalone annual letters, or the chairman's letter in the annual report or proxy. If none exist, say so.
- **Appearances.** Sweep these each time:
  1. the IR events calendar;
  2. the earnings-day CEO/CFO media hit;
  3. podcasts and non-financial shows, where CEOs are least guarded;
  4. conference transcripts.
- **Attribution.** Attribute every quote to where it was said: an off-script remark carries different weight from a scripted one.
- **None found.** If a window had no appearances, say so explicitly. An absence that goes unstated looks the same as one nobody checked.

## 7. Risks: the materiality filter

§6 covers only the risks that matter to the *decision*. Drop the boilerplate: "revenue could fluctuate", generic cyber risk, generic key-person risk.

Keep a risk only if it passes at least one test:
1. it is materially worse here than at peers;
2. it is **not priced in**, and you say so explicitly;
3. it is tied to a quantified kill criterion;
4. it is tied to a large discretionary bet with an uncertain outcome.

Name any reflexive loop or ruin path (`valuation.md` sections 7–8). Mark resolved risks `~~struck~~ ✅` with the date.

## 8. BAIT

Source: Mauboussin, *Who Is on the Other Side?* (2019). BAIT names *why* a price might differ from value. It never sets a verb or a zone; the hurdle does that.

| Lens | Question | Signals |
|---|---|---|
| **B**ehavioral | Is fear or enthusiasm running ahead of the operating data? | A drawdown into a narrative; capitulation; elevated short interest (>10% elevated, >20% extreme) |
| **A**nalytical | What is consensus missing or mis-weighting? | Segment mix, a capital-intensity shift, owner earnings vs. reported EPS, a misread reinvestment rate |
| **I**nformational | What do primary sources show that summaries don't? | Transcript detail, footnotes, appearances, scuttlebutt |
| **T**echnical | Is non-fundamental flow moving the price? | Index changes, forced selling, spin-off orphans, buyback mechanics |

Rate each lens Strong, Moderate or Weak. The Verdict cell shows the overlap: `None`, `Single (B)`, `Double (B+A)`, `Triple (B+A+I)`, `Quad`. Justify a lens in §7 only where the rating isn't obvious.

## 9. Kill criteria and the premortem

- **Premortem** (Klein). Before writing the Bear, assume it is five years from now and the stock has returned nothing. Write the three most likely reasons. These feed the Bear's mechanism, §6, and the tests.
- **Kill criteria** (Duke, *Quit*). There are exactly three tests. Each one is a *state* (a measurable threshold), a *source*, a *date*, and a **pre-committed consequence**:

  > **[Claim being tested].** Test: [metric and threshold], in [source], by [date]. **If Fail →** [scenario-probability shift or target change, and the verb it produces].

  - Compute the consequence when you write the test. For example: "move 10 points from Base to Bear → PW return 12.2% → Watch."
  - In Mode B, apply it exactly as written. Override it only with evidence that did not exist when the test was written, and name that evidence.
  - Why: deciding the consequence while you are not yet attached removes the "one sentence explaining why not" escape hatch, which is where consistency bias and sunk cost do their damage.
- **A test is not a hazard.** A test is a disconfirming observation with a date; a hazard belongs in §6.

## 10. Bias check before a verb change

Run this whenever the new verb differs from a previous report's. Answer each question in a clause, and print the result as one line in §7.

1. **Incentive:** does any input pushing the change come from someone paid to hold that view (guidance, sell-side, an activist)?
2. **Consistency:** would I write this verb if there were no previous report? Conversely, am I defending the old verb only because I wrote it?
3. **Social proof:** is the change following analyst clusters or the price rather than the business?
4. **Deprival:** am I reacting to a fresh loss or gain on the last call?
5. **Availability:** is one quarter or one headline carrying more weight than the five-year record?

Also state whether the change was driven by **evidence** (a test resolved, new numbers) or by **price** (a zone crossed). Both are legitimate; the reader should know which.
