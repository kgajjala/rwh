# 7 Powers — Moat Mechanism, Trend and Profit Pool

**Source**: Hamilton Helmer, *7 Powers: The Foundations of Business Strategy* (2016). Supplemented by Porter (five forces, value chain) for the profit-pool line and Mauboussin, *The Economics of Customer Businesses*, for the customer block.
**Where applied**: §3 of every ticker page. The Morningstar rating (Wide / Narrow / None) stays as the summary word; the Power names *why*, and the trend says *which way it is moving*.

---

## The seven mechanisms

A Power is a condition that lets a company earn persistent returns above competitors. Each pairs a **benefit** (why it pays) with a **barrier** (why rivals cannot copy it). Name at most two per company; if you need three, the moat is probably brand plus habit and you are overcounting.

| Power | Benefit | Barrier | Test that it is real |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Scale economies** | Unit cost falls with volume | A challenger must accept losses to reach scale | Cost per unit declines with size; the largest player has the highest margin in the category |
| **Network economies** | Each user makes the product worth more to others | Challenger must build liquidity on both sides at once | Take rate or pricing holds while volume grows; multi-homing is low or asymmetric |
| **Counter-positioning** | A new model the incumbent cannot adopt without damaging its own business | Incumbent's rational refusal, not its inability | Incumbent has visibly declined to copy; margins of the challenger rise as it scales |
| **Switching costs** | Customer loses value by leaving | Embedded data, workflow, training, integration | Retention and net revenue retention above 100%; price increases stick |
| **Branding** | Higher willingness to pay for an objectively similar product | Decades of consistent signals; cannot be bought quickly | Sustained price premium with stable or rising volume share |
| **Cornered resource** | Preferential access to an asset others cannot get | Patent, license, location, a specific team, allocation privilege | The asset is named, dated, and has a known expiry |
| **Process power** | Embedded organizational routines that lower cost or raise quality | Takes years to build; opaque from outside | Margin or throughput advantage persists after competitors copy the visible product |

Common mislabels: distribution reach without cost advantage is not scale; a loyalty program without a price premium is not branding; a first mover without embedded data has no switching cost.

## Trend

Required call in §3: **widening · stable · narrowing**, with the number that decides it. Evidence, in order of weight: pricing power (last increase vs. volume), share, retention or churn, gross margin against the closest peer, the competitor set's capacity (entering or leaving). LULU's page reads "Narrow, eroding" because gross margin fell 360 bps like-for-like while a private rival grew; that is the shape of a trend call.

## Profit pool

One line: **who in the value chain captures the margin, and which way it is moving.** Compressed five forces. Ask who has the power: suppliers (a vendor at 30% of purchases with no long-term contract), buyers (a retailer with shelf control), rivals (price as the only lever), entrants (private label, a funded startup), substitutes (a category the customer can leave). The Nike-to-DICK'S relationship is a supplier-power line; PepsiCo's DSD network is a buyer-power line against retailers; Uber's driver supply is a supplier-power line that autonomy would erase.

## Customer block

Wherever the unit is a customer, the page states, in a few lines or a small table:

- **Who buys and what job the product does** — plain words, one sentence.
- **Retention or repeat** — churn, cohort retention, repeat rate, or the disclosed proxy (loyalty share of sales, member share of bookings).
- **Pricing power evidence** — the last price increase and what volume did.
- **Acquisition economics** — CAC and LTV where disclosed; payback period; member vs. non-member spend.

Mauboussin's frame: a customer business is worth the sum of its cohorts. Rising retention and stable CAC compound; falling retention with rising CAC is a business buying its growth, however fast the top line moves.
