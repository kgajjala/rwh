On July 29 Chipotle did what it had failed to do for most of 2025: it raised its guidance. Second-quarter comparable sales rose 2.2%, transactions grew for the second quarter in a row, and the full-year outlook moved from "about flat" to low-single-digit growth. The stock closed at $34.25 and traded at $36.25 after hours. It peaked at $37.03 on September 14.

By October 23 it was $27.50, the lowest since January 2023. In five weeks it had lost 26% while CAVA, the competitor investors worry about most, lost 2% and consumer stocks were flat. Nothing in the business had been reported since July. What changed was a run of signals around it. Industry traffic data for August came in weak. A takeover rumor arrived and was withdrawn. A respected analyst said October looked soft. Then the third quarter came out on October 28: comps of 2.1% and a third straight quarter of growing transactions, with a restaurant margin of 23.3% and a fourth-quarter margin guided under 22%. The stock rose 9% the next day and closed at $31.89 on Friday.

::chart since_niccol_exit

The chart starts on August 12, 2024, the last day before Brian Niccol's move to Starbucks was announced. Since then Chipotle shareholders have lost about 43% of their money, CAVA's about 35%, while Starbucks and the S&P 500 are up 42% and 45%. Over the same period Chipotle's revenue grew from $11.3 billion in 2024 to $12.7 billion over the last twelve months, and EPS went from $1.11 to $1.07. So the whole loss is the multiple, which fell from about 54 times trailing earnings to 30. Mr. Market used to price Chipotle as a company that would compound for decades at 20%. He now prices it as a good restaurant chain with a traffic problem.

The question this piece tries to answer is which of those is closer to the truth, and whether $31.89 pays you for the uncertainty. I start with the business, then spend most of the time on traffic: whether the 2026 recovery is a turn or a compare. Then comes the margin, where beef and freight have run ahead of price. Then management, including a buyback that has spent most of the company's cash and a takeover rumor that came and went. I finish with valuation and the three things that would change my answer. Two of the three have been read once, on October 28, and the next reading is in February.

## Business Overview {#overview}

Chipotle owns and runs all of its roughly 4,200 restaurants in the United States, Canada and Europe, plus a handful of partner-operated restaurants in the Middle East. It has no franchisees, no debt and one reportable segment. Its unit of value is the restaurant. The average one does $3.1 million of sales a year at a restaurant-level margin of about 24%, and about 80% of new ones include a Chipotlane, the drive-through pickup window for digital orders.

::table results

Two things in the table matter for what follows. Revenue growth has been carried by new restaurants: 334 opened in 2025 and about 350–370 this year. The comparable restaurants have done much less. Comps went from +7.4% in 2024 to −1.7% in 2025, the first annual decline since the 2016 food-safety crisis, and back to about +1.6% in the first nine months of 2026. Meanwhile restaurant-level margin has fallen from 26.7% to 23.9% over the same nine months, and the average restaurant now sells less than it did two years ago.

::chart cost_stack

A few accounting points. Chipotle reports restaurant-level margin as a non-GAAP measure. It is revenue less food, labor, occupancy and other restaurant costs, before G&A and depreciation. The company owns no real estate; its $5.4 billion of operating leases are the main fixed obligation. Q1 and Q2 2026 included legal reserves and restructuring costs, which is why GAAP operating margin dropped to 12.9% in Q1 while the adjusted restaurant margin was 23.7%. EPS here is GAAP and includes stock compensation, which is about 1% of revenue. All per-share figures reflect the 50-for-1 split in June 2024.

## Traffic: A Turn or a Compare? {#traffic}

Let me start with where things stand. The Q3 column is the new one, and it is the best traffic number Chipotle has printed since 2024.

::table drivers

### Management's narrative

Management's case is that the "Recipe for Growth" plan launched in February is working. It has five parts: better throughput, more menu innovation, a relaunched Rewards program, hospitality, and group and catering occasions. On the July 29 call, CEO Scott Boatwright said Chipotle had "taken share in each month of 2026" and saw its "highest year-over-year gains in wallet share last quarter since 2024." He pointed to affordability scores "better in Q2 than they've been in probably the past couple of years," and to a younger and lower-income customer base that had improved the most, the cohort that had been hurt worst in 2025. On value, he made the point that matters for the margin debate: "value isn't just about discounting and price point." His argument is that a faster line, bigger portions and new items do more for perceived value than price cuts.

CFO Adam Rymer gave the near-term picture. Traffic improved through the second quarter and into early July. Then an industry-wide Cyclospora outbreak, in produce Chipotle doesn't serve, hit restaurant visits broadly: "we did see a softening, call it about 200 basis points or so right around the issue." He guided Q3 comps to "somewhere around a plus 1%" assuming that drag continued, which implies an underlying trend near 3%. He also said "comparisons become more difficult in the third quarter as we lap increased promotional activity in the prior year."

The supporting evidence is real. Linebackers, a staff member who helps the line move at peak, are deployed in over 70% of restaurants. Restaurants with the new equipment package serve two to three more entrees in their peak 15 minutes. General-manager turnover is at a multiyear low. Rymer said limited-time offers typically deliver "hundreds of basis points of trans lift," and Boatwright said a guest who tries an LTO is worth materially more over their lifetime.

### Poking holes in it

The first hole is the base. Q3 transactions rose 0.8% against a 0.8% decline a year earlier, so the two-year stack went from −3.9% to about zero. That is real progress, and CEO Scott Boatwright said September comps were up 3.8% with very little promotion. But the compare was easy, the quarter needed promotional labor and marketing to hold through the mid-July industry slump, and "October trends have softened, consistent with the broader industry," as CFO Adam Rymer put it. The fourth quarter is guided to comps of about +2% with about 3% of that from price, which leaves transactions near +0.5% against a −3.2% compare that should be the easiest of the year. TD Cowen made this point on October 21 when it downgraded the stock, and the company's own guide now agrees with it.

The second hole is cost. David Palmer of Evercore put it plainly on the call: "it feels like Chipotle is doing a lot and spending a lot to get the 1% to 2% same-store sales growth lately." Boatwright's answer was that the initiatives are showing "green shoots of positive momentum." That is probably true, but labor rose to 25% of revenue from 24.7% partly because of extra hospitality staff, and the LTO calendar now has to keep running to hold traffic. Boatwright himself said of LTOs, "I do think there's a point of diminishing returns."

The third hole is CAVA. In the quarter ended July 12, CAVA's same-restaurant sales rose 9.0% with traffic up 5.3%. Chipotle's traffic grew 1.0%. CAVA is about a ninth of Chipotle's size, so this is not a story of customers being taken in bulk. It is a sign that the fast-casual bowl, a format Chipotle invented, is growing and Chipotle is not getting its share of the growth. The share that CAVA wins at the edge is exactly the incremental customer Chipotle needs to grow traffic.

The fourth hole is the restaurant itself. Average restaurant sales fell 3.4% in 2025 and another 1.3% in the year to June. Rymer said new openings take about 100 basis points a year from comparable sales through cannibalization. That is the right trade if each new restaurant earns about 60% cash-on-cash in year two, which it does. But it also means the long-term goal of $4 million AUVs set in February 2024 is getting further away, not closer.

::table restaurant

The illustration is the debate in one row. An average Chipotle earned about $900,000 of restaurant-level profit in mid-2024 and about $725,000 in Q3 2026, with nearly all of the difference coming from margin. My Base gets back to about $820,000 by 2031 through price that matches food inflation and flat traffic. Management's long-term goal of a $4 million AUV at a margin "approaching 30%" would be worth about $1.15 million, 59% more than today. That goal needs roughly 29% more sales per restaurant. In a format where traffic has fallen for two years, it is a Bull assumption, not a Base.

Where I come out: the traffic recovery is real, no longer thin by the two-year measure, and still dependent on a heavy promotional and marketing calendar. The Q3 leg of my first test passed. The Q4 leg asks for transactions of at least +1.5% against −3.2%, and the guide implies less than that. If Q4 comes in near +0.5%, 2026 will have been a turn that needed spending to hold, and I will move weight to the Bear.

### Three rings of profit {#rings}

::table rings

The rings make the risk clear. Almost all of the profit sits in the comparable U.S. restaurants, which is where the traffic debate lives. New restaurants are earning well and international is a small option. Unlike Starbucks or McDonald's, Chipotle has no royalty stream or licensed business to cushion a traffic problem. That is the cost of owning every restaurant, and it is why the multiple moves so much with the comp.

## Margin: Can Price Catch Beef? {#margin}

The second debate is narrower and more mechanical. Restaurant-level margin was 27.4% in Q2 2025 and 25.2% in Q2 2026. The 10-Q gives the walk. Food, beverage and packaging rose 0.8 points of revenue: 1.4 points of inflation, mostly beef and freight, and 0.5 points of richer portions and produce usage, offset by 0.5 from menu price and 0.5 from cheaper avocados and dairy. Labor rose 0.3 points and other operating costs 0.9 points.

Management chose not to price for it. Rymer said pricing ran "around 1.6%" in Q2, would rise to "the mid-2% range in Q3," and that "from Q4 forward, we expect those 2 to match," meaning price and food inflation. For the year he expects pricing near the high end of 1–2%. That is a deliberate choice: Chipotle's 2022–24 price increases were large, traffic fell in 2025, and Boatwright doesn't want to repeat it while the recovery is fragile. I think it is the right choice for the long run. It is also why EPS has been flat for two years while revenue grew 10%.

The arithmetic is simple. Each point of restaurant margin is worth about $130 million of pre-tax profit at this year's revenue, or about 8 cents of EPS, about 7% of earnings. Getting from 24% back to the 26.7% of 2024 is worth about 21 cents. My Base assumes the margin gets back to 24% and stays there; the Q3 miss took the 25% I had before out of it. The Bull assumes 27% with traffic growth on top. The Bear assumes 21.5%, which would be the margin of a Chipotle that keeps paying for traffic it doesn't get.

The risk is that matching inflation isn't enough. Beef prices have been rising for three years on a shrinking U.S. herd, and tariffs on Mexican produce and packaging are flagged in the 10-K. Labor is the other half. Several states have raised restaurant minimum wages, and the hospitality model adds labor by design. Q3 came in at 23.3% against 24.5% a year earlier, and the fourth quarter is guided just under 22%, about 150 bps lower than last year. Half of that gap is a prior-year gift-card benefit and about 30 bps is the Smoked Brisket marketing push, so the underlying fall is smaller. But Rymer also said pricing is now only "in line with inflation," about 3% in Q4 and about 2% for the year, and that 2027 inflation should run low to mid single digits. My second test asked for 24.0% in Q3 and it failed, so the Base margin is capped at 24% from here. Margin recovery now has to come from transactions, which Rymer said will expand margins "as we drive additional transaction growth," and not from price.

## Management, Capital Allocation and Incentives {#mgmt}

Scott Boatwright became CEO in November 2024, after Niccol left. He ran restaurant operations from 2017 and through Niccol's tenure, which makes him the architect of much of what worked: throughput, the Chipotlane rollout and the operations playbook behind the 2018–24 recovery. His first two years as CEO were hard. 2025 guidance was cut three times, from "low to mid-single digit" comp growth to a 1.7% decline, and the stock lost a third of its value. 2026 has been better so far. Adam Rymer is CFO. Chipotle also has a new chief brand officer and chief digital officer this year, and in September it added Sabir Sami, the former CEO of KFC, to the board.

The obvious comparison is Niccol's own record at Chipotle (2018–24), covered in my Starbucks piece. It was a 768% total return built almost entirely on earnings: margin from 6% to 17%, and revenue more than doubled. Boatwright inherited that machine at its peak margin and has so far defended unit growth while margin and traffic slipped.

### The cash

::table capital

Chipotle is simple to read. Over 2021–25 it generated $5.9 billion of free cash flow after funding about 1,360 new restaurants from its own cash, and returned $5.3 billion of it through buybacks. It has no debt and pays no dividend. The share count fell 5.8%. That is a model of restaurant capital allocation: the business funds its own growth and returns the rest.

What changed in 2025 is the pace. Chipotle bought back $2.4 billion in 2025 and $1.35 billion in the first half of 2026, well above free cash flow. Cash and investments fell from $2.29 billion at the end of 2024 to $0.78 billion in June.

::table buybacks

The pattern is countercyclical in the good sense: Chipotle bought more as the price fell, $742 million at $34 in Q4 2025 against $436 million at $50 in Q2. That is how a buyback should behave. But the average cost of about $39 is well above today's $31.89, so the $3.9 billion has lost about $0.7 billion of value so far. In Q3 the company bought only $110 million at $34.05 because a blackout kept it out of the market, and cash and investments rebuilt to $1.0 billion. From here buybacks can run only at the pace of free cash flow, about $1.5 billion a year, or Chipotle has to borrow. That is still 4% of the market value a year, which is a good deal at 25 times earnings. But the extra support the stock got from spending the cash pile is over.

I grade capital allocation **Outsider-leaning**. The buybacks were sized against weakness, there is no M&A and no debt, and the growth capex earns the returns described above. It falls short of Outsider because the buyback outran free cash flow and drained the cash pile at prices that now look high.

Then there was October 8. The Financial Times reported that Starbucks had worked with advisers on a possible bid for Chipotle, and the stock rose 6% to $32.68 in a day. Four days later a Starbucks spokesperson told CNBC the company is "not buying Chipotle," and the stock gave it all back within two sessions. Chipotle itself said nothing. I don't think the episode tells us much about Chipotle's value. It does show that the stock had been carrying a little takeover hope, and that is now gone. Niccol knows this business better than anyone and judged it cheap enough to look at. That counts for something too.

### The incentives

::table incentives

The plan is well aimed at restaurant economics. The annual bonus pays equally on comparable sales and restaurant cash-flow margin, which is precisely the tension in the margin chapter: management is paid to grow traffic and to protect the margin, and can't simply buy one with the other. The performance shares pay on cash flow from the existing base, excluding new restaurants, so management can't hit the target just by opening more. A TSR cap stops a big payout if the stock lags. The 2023–25 grant paid 273% on the strong 2023–24 years, and the 2025 company factor paid 40% in a bad year. That is how pay should move.

What is missing is return on capital. That matters less at Chipotle than at most companies, because new restaurants are funded from cash and earn very high returns. It would matter more if management ever needed to choose between a buyback and a remodel program. Boatwright has hinted at "between $100,000 and $300,000 per restaurant" for the first aesthetic remodels in the brand's history.

### The promises

::table promises

**Guidance grade: Mixed.** 2025 was a miss: comp guidance was cut three times and ended at −1.7% against an initial low-to-mid-single-digit growth target. 2026 has been a beat so far, raised from flat to low single digits. The long-term promises are going the wrong way. Unit growth is on track, but AUV and margin are both moving away from the 2024 targets.

Is Boatwright the right manager? On operations and unit growth, I think yes. He built much of the system that made Chipotle what it is, and the 2026 metrics, turnover, throughput and new-unit returns, are improving. The open question is brand. Can he make the bowl feel worth $13–15 to a younger customer who now has CAVA down the street, without paying for traffic with margin? The next two quarters will say a lot.

## Valuation and Model Assumptions {#val}

I model Chipotle as one restaurant business. Revenue grows from units and comps. Restaurant-level margin is the main margin input, and G&A, depreciation and pre-opening costs come off as "corporate and other" at about 9.5–10% of revenue. Then come interest income, tax at 24.5% and buybacks. FY2026 is calibrated to about $12.9 billion of revenue, a 23.4% restaurant margin (nine months actual, Q4 at the guided just under 22%) and $1.06 of EPS. Capitalizing owner earnings with no growth gives only $12–14 a share, so about 58% of today's price is paying for the restaurants still to be opened. I am comfortable with that, because the unit pipeline is the most proven thing about the company.

**Bias check.** The previous page said "Watch / Initiate (scaled $27–32)" for a non-holder at $28.70. That page used a margin-of-safety entry 20% below its value. The new verb is Watch with entry at [[entry]]. (1) Incentive: the inputs come from company filings; TD Cowen's downgrade is context and moves no case. (2) Consistency: with no previous page I would write Watch at this return. (3) Social proof: the analyst consensus target is $43.55, so I am leaning against it, not with it. (4) Deprival: no fresh loss drives this. (5) Availability: the five-year record carries the case, and one quarter does not. The change comes from **method and evidence**, not price. The hurdle-derived entry replaced a margin-of-safety discount, the two-year traffic stack is weaker than the headline comps, and the margin fell 220 bps.

## Final Words {#final}

Chipotle is a better business than its stock price says, and a worse one than it was two years ago. It has no debt and funds about 350 new restaurants a year from its own cash at very high returns. It still earns 22% on its capital and has a decade of growth ahead in North America alone. But its average restaurant now sells less than in 2024, its margin is more than three points lower, and its traffic over two years is down while a smaller rival's is growing fast. Mr. Market has gone from paying 54 times earnings for the first story to about 30 times for the second.

At $31.89 he is paying more than I would. The probability-weighted return is [[pw_return]] a year, below the [[compounding]] the business compounds at a flat multiple because the multiple falls from about 30 times to 24, and well short of the 13% hurdle. The rebound since October 23 has made Chipotle fairly priced to full, not cheap. The entry is about [[entry]], about 29% below today. For a holder the answer is Hold. The balance sheet removes ruin risk, and the business will keep growing even if traffic stays flat.

What would change my mind? The next answer comes in February. Q4 transactions of at least +1.5% against −3.2% would make 2026 a turn and not a promotion-supported compare. A Q4 restaurant margin of at least 21.8% and a 2027 margin consistent with 24% or better would say price has stopped losing to beef. And AUV holding at $3.10 million or better, against $3.106 million now, would show that 350 openings a year are not eating the old restaurants. All three make the case for more than $31.89. Any one failing makes it for less.
